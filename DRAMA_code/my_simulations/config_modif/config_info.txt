(lists are used for parametric analyses):

RUN make config to see all the possible values and the default values.


based on keplerian coordinates 
element1 → Semi-major axis (a), in kilometers
element2 → Eccentricity (e), unitless
element3 → Inclination (i), in degrees
element4 → Right ascension of the ascending node (Ω), in degrees
element5 → Argument of perigee (ω), in degrees
element6 → True anomaly (ν), in degrees


Several factors together determine the latitude and longitude at which a satellite or fragment actually re-enters (and where it ultimately impacts or burns up):

Initial orbital elements:
    Inclination (element3) sets the maximum latitude band. For example, a 50°-inclined orbit can only re-enter between 50° S and 50° N.
    RAAN (element4) & argument of perigee (element5) combined with the re-entry date/time determine where the ground track crosses the equator and where perigee/apogee occur relative to the rotating Earth.
    True anomaly at break-up (element6) fixes the precise orbital position where the object begins its rapid descent.
Earth’s rotation
As the satellite decelerates in the upper atmosphere, the Earth continues to turn beneath it. A slower, more gradual descent (due to low drag or low atmospheric density) means the ground impact point shifts eastward.
Atmospheric drag and density
    Drag coefficient & cross-section: higher drag or larger area causes earlier slowing and a shorter longitude path before burn-up or impact.
    Atmospheric model & density scaling (solar cycle, geomagnetic activity, winds): denser atmosphere—whether from high solar activity (F10.7, ap indices) or strong upper-atmospheric winds—accelerates deceleration and alters where re-entry “fires” occur.
Space-weather and winds
    Solar indices (e.g. ap, F10.7) change neutral density at re-entry altitude.
    Upper-atmosphere winds can drift fragments laterally, especially near peak aerodynamic heating.
Fragmentation behavior
    Break-up altitude & grid resolution (voxelatorMode, resolution): finer fragmentation spreads pieces over a wider ground track in lat/long.
    Fragment attitude after break-up: some pieces may have shapes or orientations that generate more lift or drag, shifting their trajectories.
Time of day (orbital phase relative to Earth rotation)
Even the same orbit will map to very different ground tracks depending on whether re-entry occurs at local dawn, midday, or midnight, because the launch of the ascending/descending nodes relative to the sun shifts with each orbit.


In practice, one conducts a Monte-Carlo re-entry simulation, varying:

    Small uncertainties on the six Keplerian elements (a, e, i, Ω, ω, ν)
    Drag coefficient and cross-section
    Solar and geomagnetic indices (density)
    Exact breakup timing


NOTE:  the break up altitude is in the dpz file.
